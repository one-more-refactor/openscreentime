The house clock.

OpenScreenTime is the clock on the kitchen wall, not a cop at the door. Everyone can read it, nobody has to wind it, and it says the time plainly — to children, to parents, and to anyone keeping time for themselves.

{{mark:size=220}}
01

The idea

One object explains every decision in the product. A wall clock has five properties, and each one is a promise we make.

{{icon:family}}

Fair

Everyone reads the same clock. A child sees exactly what a parent sees about them, and the parent's own time hangs on the same wall.

{{icon:home}}

Set it once

You hang it and it runs for years. Nothing to maintain, nothing to babysit, nothing to check on.

{{icon:clock}}

Quiet

A clock doesn't nag. You glance at it. It only speaks when a person is actually needed.

{{icon:check}}

Honest

A clock doesn't lie and doesn't argue. When time is up, it says so plainly — calm, kind, firm, in that order.

{{icon:person}}

Yours too

A clock works for one person. It is also the clock on your own desk, for the time you set yourself.

Set it once.
It keeps time.

The line. Six words, two readings: it runs on its own, and it holds the time you agreed on. It answers the owner's complaint directly — this should not feel like a project you maintain. The runner-up, “Fair time, for everyone at home”, survives as the descriptor under the wordmark where a second sentence is needed.

02

The mark

The activity ring you already have, read as a clock face. One added detail — a tick at twelve o'clock, the start line of every day — is what separates it from a progress spinner.

  • Box64 units. Ring ø 44 (r 22), centred. The ring is 69% of the box.
  • Stroke7 units — 16% of the ring diameter. Heavier than the data rings on purpose, so it holds at 16 px.
  • The tick3 wide × 11 long, round caps, centred on the track at twelve o'clock, overhanging the track by 2 on each side. Ink. Always painted last, on top of the arc.
  • The arcStarts flush under the tick (butt), sweeps clockwise, ends in a round cap (a disc, r 3.5). The static mark is fixed at 40% = 144° — a morning, most of the day still ahead.
  • Colourtrack #d5d1c9 · arc #2e7d46 · tick #1e1c19. Dark: #423d37 / #46b06a / #f3f0ea. One-colour: everything currentColor, track at 25% opacity.
  • In the productData rings keep these proportions on a lighter stroke: 9% of ø on hero rings, 8% at 56–72 px, 7% at 28 px. The tick appears on every ring 40 px and larger; below that the ring is too small to carry it.

The ring is time used today, filling clockwise from the tick. The number beside it may say what is left, because that is what a person asks. A number inside a ring must fit the inner circle with room to spare: up to three characters at 0.29 × ø, anything longer (“1 h 12”) drops to 0.2 × ø — never abbreviate to “1:12”. The ring never depletes, never spins, and never counts anything but a day.

Colour and type

Unchanged — the palette and the typeface were right; the execution was not. Colour is for hierarchy: most of any screen is paper, white and ink. Green is time and the one main action. Amber is a transition. Red is stop, and a healthy day has none.

04

The icon set

Forty-one monoline icons on a 24 px grid: 2 px stroke, round caps and joins, currentColor, no fills except three tiny dots. The geometry rhymes with the clock — circles, arcs, and the twelve-tick wherever time is the subject. Each is a standalone file in icons/, drawn with plain primitives so React and the egui SVG loader render the same shape.

Rules: the stroke never changes weight, even at 16 px (the grid is 24, so 16 px is a 1.33 px stroke — still crisp). Nothing is filled except the three dots in more, info and warning. No cog: settings is a pair of sliders, because you set a limit like a dial, not like machinery. No shield anywhere — a shield says guard, and this is a clock. The stop icon is a rounded square, not a hand or an octagon.

06

Voice

Calm, kind, firm — in that order, never traded against each other. The fact is always stated; the kindness is in how, and in what comes next. Sentence case. Second person. One verb per screen.
